Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Compare common FTC linear-motion approaches including slides, elevator stages, belts, strings/cables, lead screws, and rack-and-pinion.
  • Explain continuous and cascading rigging concepts and the relationship between spool/pulley motion and stage travel.
  • Design bearing/alignment structure that resists racking and binding.
  • Estimate linear speed from spool diameter and rotational speed.
  • Use sensors, limits, homing, gravity compensation, and service access to make a lift reliable.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Guide Structure

Slides or rails constrain motion. Parallelism, bearing spacing, stiffness, fastener access, and protection from side loads determine whether the mechanism moves smoothly.

Rigging

String/cable, belt, or chain can synchronize stages. Rigging geometry must be documented because a small routing error can create opposite motion, unequal travel, or overload.

Continuous

In a continuous-style multi-stage lift, one stage's motion drives the next through a continuous routing. It can package compactly but requires careful tension and routing.

Cascade

Cascade rigging drives stages in a coordinated relationship. It can make stage motion predictable but places specific loads on line/belt anchors and pulleys.

Spool / Pulley

The effective radius converts rotational speed/torque into linear motion/force. Layering string on a spool can change effective diameter if not controlled.

Control

Encoders, limit switches, current sensing, homing, soft limits, PID, and feedforward can improve repeatability—but they rely on a mechanically trustworthy lift.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Racking

A stage can bind when force is applied off-center or rails are not parallel. Wide bearing spacing and symmetric loading help resist twisting.

Overlap

Stages need adequate engagement/overlap for stiffness. Maximum extension with minimal overlap may score in CAD but flex badly on the field.

Cable/Line Wear

Inspect line, knots/terminations, pulleys, anchors, and abrasion points. Design replacement access before the line breaks.

Limits

Use physical geometry and software limits to keep the mechanism from driving beyond safe travel. A homed reference gives the software a known origin.

Gravity

An elevator needs upward force to hold position. Appropriate feedforward/counterbalance can reduce feedback effort and heating.

Center of Gravity

Extending a lift changes robot stability. Test acceleration, turning, and stopping in the highest/heaviest scoring state.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Build the guide/rail structure square before attaching rigging. Do not use line tension to force misaligned rails into place.
  • Document rigging with a diagram showing fixed anchors, moving anchors, pulley direction, and expected stage travel.
  • Use witness marks or measured references for re-tensioning after line replacement.
  • Test lift current/temperature and cycle time at the actual maximum game-piece load.
  • Add a homing routine that moves slowly toward a trustworthy reference and then establishes position.
  • Protect the lift from software commands beyond physical travel using soft limits and safe presets.
  • Cycle the mechanism hundreds of times and inspect line wear, loose pulleys, bearings, and fasteners.
  • Practice replacing the rigging and verify that the robot can be returned to known calibration afterward.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
