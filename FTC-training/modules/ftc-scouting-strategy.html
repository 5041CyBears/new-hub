Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Collect scouting data that helps the drive team and alliance partners make decisions rather than simply accumulating statistics.
  • Separate repeatable capability, actual match contribution, reliability, penalties, autonomous performance, and role fit.
  • Build a match plan around complementary alliance strengths and realistic cycle timing.
  • Recognize sampling bias, double-counting, context, defense effects, and unreliable subjective ratings.
  • Communicate scouting insights quickly and respectfully before and during events.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Match Scouting

Record what a robot actually does in matches: autonomous actions, cycles, scoring locations, failures, penalties, defense, recovery, and endgame behavior.

Pit Scouting

Use the pit for facts difficult to observe from the stands: drivetrain/mechanism architecture, autonomous options, dimensions, preferred role, and repair status. Verify rather than interrogate.

Capability vs. Output

A robot may be capable of five cycles but complete only two because of traffic, alliance role, defense, or a failure. Capture both capability and observed consistency.

Reliability

A slightly lower-scoring robot that completes almost every match can be more valuable than a high-ceiling robot with frequent disconnects or mechanism failures.

Role Fit

Alliance value depends on combination. Two robots that need the exact same scoring zone may interfere; a complementary defender, specialist, or reliable autonomous may add more.

Match Plan

A useful plan identifies autonomous paths, first scoring actions, traffic lanes, cycle roles, defense response, endgame timing, and communication.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Define Metrics

Every scout should know exactly what counts as a cycle, attempted score, successful score, disable, penalty, or autonomous action.

Avoid Double Counting

Do not add a derived total to all of the components that already created that total. Decide whether data is raw events, calculated contribution, or an external metric.

Context Matters

Opponent strength, partner role, defense, field traffic, penalties, and robot damage explain why one match may differ from normal.

Sample Size

Early-event averages are unstable. Combine quantitative data with recent trend, reliability notes, and direct observation.

Subjective Fields

If using ratings like 'driver skill 1–5,' define anchors. Better yet, record observable behavior such as time to recover, missed alignment, or defense escape.

Data Validation

Compare scout totals with official results when possible, flag impossible values, and resolve discrepancies rather than averaging bad data.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Pilot the scouting form during practice matches and remove fields no one uses in decisions.
  • Write a one-page metric definition guide so all scouts count the same events.
  • Use two or more scouts/validation methods for critical data when staffing allows.
  • Mark unusual context such as dead partner, heavy defense, robot damage, or field issue.
  • During match planning, lead with a short alliance-role recommendation, then the evidence.
  • Talk with alliance partners respectfully; scouting data is incomplete and teams know their own current robot state.
  • Update strategy when a robot is repaired, damaged, or changes autonomous—event data is not static.
  • After the event, ask which scouting fields actually changed decisions and simplify for next time.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
