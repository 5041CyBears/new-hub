Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Break a scoring task into game-piece acquisition, control, transfer, placement, and release functions.
  • Compare rollers, claws, arms, linkages, slides, passive guides, and compliant elements.
  • Design interfaces between mechanisms instead of optimizing each subsystem independently.
  • Prototype the riskiest assumptions with real game pieces before committing to a final architecture.
  • Balance performance with packaging, weight, serviceability, reliability, and driver recovery.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Intakes

Good intakes create a forgiving capture zone and control the game piece quickly. Compliance, funneling, roller direction, surface speed, and geometry often matter more than raw motor power.

Claws & Grippers

A claw should capture reliably across realistic game-piece variation. Think about contact points, centering, release, servo geometry, and what happens when the piece enters crooked.

Arms & Pivots

Arms provide angular reach but create gravity torque and packaging challenges. Bearing support, reduction, counterbalance, and cable routing must be designed together.

Slides & Elevators

Linear mechanisms can extend compactly but require alignment, rigging, limits, and protection from racking. Their moving mass affects stability.

Passive Geometry

Funnels, guides, ramps, hard stops, spring fingers, and compliant surfaces can solve alignment problems without extra sensors or actuators.

Transfers

A robot often fails where one mechanism hands the game piece to another. Design the transfer state and tolerances explicitly.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Requirements Before CAD

Write measurable requirements: what objects, from where, orientation variation, target location, cycle time, size envelope, and recovery conditions.

Prototype the Unknown

Do not prototype what you already know. Test uncertain game-piece interaction, compression, grip, release, geometry, or transfer behavior first.

Compliance

Soft wheels, flexures, spring-loaded rollers, or compliant mounts can tolerate variation and reduce jams, but too much compliance can reduce control.

Packaging

Reserve space for wiring, hubs, fasteners, tools, structure, and maintenance. A mechanism that only fits in CAD may not be serviceable.

Failure Recovery

Drivers need a way to reverse, open, retract, or otherwise recover from a bad pickup. Build recovery into the design and controls.

Iteration Evidence

Record dimensions, settings, videos, cycle times, jam rates, and changes. This improves both engineering decisions and the portfolio.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Sketch the entire game-piece path through the robot before designing individual mechanisms.
  • Build a crude prototype quickly and test the most uncertain interaction with actual game pieces.
  • Measure capture width, successful pickup percentage, jam frequency, release consistency, and cycle time.
  • Test game pieces at different orientations, speeds, wear levels, and entry positions.
  • Design access to common jam points and high-wear parts.
  • Add reversal or recovery controls for mechanisms that can jam.
  • Protect wires and sensor cables through the full mechanism range of motion.
  • Use video slow motion to understand bouncing, slipping, compression, and missed transfers.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
