Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Explain the purpose of FTC judging, structured interviews, pit conversations, and award evidence.
  • Give concise answers built from a direct response, specific example, evidence/result, and stop.
  • Prepare multiple students to explain technical work, team operations, outreach, and lessons authentically.
  • Use the portfolio as supporting evidence without reciting it.
  • Practice difficult follow-up questions, uncertainty, and honest acknowledgment of what a student does not know.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Structured Interview

FTC events use a formal judging process defined in current FIRST event/judging resources. Teams should review the current process guide and question bank because terminology and procedures can evolve.

Pit Conversations

Judges may seek additional information in the pits depending on the event process. Keep the pit safe, welcoming, and ready for students to explain the robot and team.

Student Ownership

Judges are trying to understand what students know, did, decided, and learned. Adult-polished language cannot replace student understanding.

Evidence

Specific examples are stronger than adjectives. Replace 'we collaborate well' with a short story showing how responsibilities, conflict, or design decisions were handled.

Coverage

Not every student needs to know every detail, but the team should cover robot design/programming, portfolio, outreach, team organization, and season growth.

Natural Delivery

Practice structure, not a memorized script. Students should be able to answer the same question in different words and handle follow-ups.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Answer Pattern

A useful structure is: direct answer → one specific example → measurable/observable result → stop. Let the judge ask the next question.

Technical Explanation

Start with function and design requirement, then explain how it works, why the team chose it, what changed, and the evidence.

Unknown Questions

It is acceptable to say 'I don't know that detail, but Sam worked on it and can explain.' Guessing damages credibility.

Team Handoffs

Students should smoothly invite the teammate with direct experience instead of interrupting or competing for airtime.

Questions From Portfolio

Any chart, claim, calculation, outreach number, or technical decision in the portfolio may invite follow-up. Know the source.

Practice Environment

Mock judging should include interruptions, unexpected questions, limited time, and students who were not the primary presenters.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Build a shared evidence bank of 2–3 concrete stories for engineering, outreach, sustainability, leadership, and teamwork.
  • Have students practice answering without slides or scripts; judges may ask questions in any order.
  • Use names and roles naturally: 'Maya tested that code; she can explain the tuning.'
  • Avoid monopolizing. Presenters should notice when another student has direct experience.
  • Answer the question asked before adding background.
  • If a judge misunderstands, clarify respectfully with evidence rather than arguing.
  • Practice explaining technical ideas to a non-specialist without removing the engineering substance.
  • After each mock interview, record only a few actionable improvements so practice does not become overwhelming.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
