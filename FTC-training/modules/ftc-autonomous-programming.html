Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Build autonomous routines as a sequence of measurable robot states rather than a chain of blind delays.
  • Use encoders, IMU heading, sensors, and localization appropriately.
  • Create timeouts and recovery behavior so a failed step does not consume the entire autonomous period.
  • Tune motion using repeatable starting conditions and recorded error.
  • Understand when simple encoder/IMU routines are enough and when a pathing/localization library is justified.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Repeatable Start

Autonomous accuracy starts before code: consistent robot placement, mechanism state, battery condition, and game-piece setup reduce uncontrolled variation.

Encoder Motion

Motor/wheel encoders estimate wheel rotation. Convert counts to mechanism or robot travel carefully and remember that wheel slip can make counts differ from actual field motion.

IMU Heading

An IMU can measure orientation and support heading control. Establish coordinate conventions and zeroing behavior so every routine agrees on positive rotation and field direction.

State Sequence

Represent autonomous as clear steps: acquire, drive, align, score, retreat. Each step should have a completion condition and preferably a timeout.

Localization

Pose estimation combines motion measurements to estimate robot position and heading. It is powerful, but poor geometry, slip, or bad sensor data can still produce a confident wrong pose.

Pathing Libraries

Road Runner, Pedro Pathing, or similar tools can provide trajectories and controllers. Use them after understanding the robot's localization and tuning requirements.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Time Is Not Position

A command such as 'drive for 1.4 seconds' changes with battery voltage, friction, collisions, and load. Use time only where precision is not required or as a safety timeout.

Counts to Distance

Encoder math depends on counts per revolution, gearing, and wheel/spool circumference. Measure the real result and calibrate rather than treating nominal wheel diameter as perfect.

Heading Hold

Straight driving often improves when translational motion is paired with heading correction.

Timeouts

Every sensor-driven step can fail. A timeout allows the sequence to stop, skip, park, or choose a safer fallback instead of hanging forever.

Tolerance

Do not require an impossible exact measurement. Define a target tolerance appropriate to scoring geometry and sensor noise.

Telemetry & Logs

Record target, measured pose/heading, error, step/state, and output. Without these, 'it missed left' is not enough information to diagnose why.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Create a physical starting fixture, tape marks, or repeatable reference so every test begins from the same pose.
  • Test one autonomous segment at a time before combining the full routine.
  • Show the current autonomous state on telemetry so the team immediately knows where a failure occurred.
  • Use conservative speed until the route is geometrically reliable; then increase speed while measuring error.
  • Measure final position error over many runs, not just the best run.
  • Test with low and high battery states, realistic game-piece loads, and normal field-contact disturbances.
  • Give scoring actions a timeout and define what the robot should do if the action fails.
  • Keep at least one simpler safe autonomous option for events when a complex sensor or mechanism is unreliable.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
