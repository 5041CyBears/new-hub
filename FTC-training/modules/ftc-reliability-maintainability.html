Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Design robot systems to survive repeated match cycles, impacts, vibration, transportation, and pit repair.
  • Identify common failure modes in fasteners, shafts, hubs, bearings, belts/chains, wiring, printed parts, and software configuration.
  • Use preventive inspection, witness marks, spares, and service access to reduce downtime.
  • Apply simple risk prioritization to focus effort on failures with high severity, likelihood, or poor detectability.
  • Perform root-cause fixes rather than repeatedly repairing symptoms.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Retention

A shaft, bolt, gear, hub, connector, or pulley needs a deliberate retention method. Vibration and reversal expose parts that rely only on friction or wishful thinking.

Load Paths

Support forces through bearings, structure, and joints intentionally. Cantilevered loads, unsupported shafts, and weak printed layer orientation create predictable failures.

Wire Reliability

Protect connectors from tension and moving mechanisms. Label circuits and leave enough access to replace components quickly.

Serviceability

Design common wear parts so they can be inspected and replaced without removing half the robot.

Preventive Checks

Use a repeatable inspection list based on the robot's actual failure history: fasteners, wheels, line, belts, hubs, connectors, slides, sensor mounts, and battery.

Root Cause

If the same thing fails repeatedly, ask why. A stronger replacement part may not fix misalignment, overload, poor retention, or an inaccessible maintenance process.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Witness Marks

Paint/marker across a fastener and adjacent part can make rotation or movement visible during quick checks.

Thread Retention

Use appropriate legal retention methods for the material/joint. Threadlocker is not a substitute for correct fastener engagement and torque, and some plastics require care.

Shaft Retention

Collars, clips, bolts/washers, retaining rings, or captured geometry should prevent shafts and hubs from migrating.

Printed Parts

Orient layers around expected load, use adequate walls, avoid sharp stress concentrations, and keep spares for known wear/impact parts.

Connector Access

A connector that is impossible to reach turns a 30-second diagnosis into a 10-minute disassembly.

Failure Log

Record component, symptom, match/test context, immediate fix, suspected root cause, and permanent correction. Patterns become obvious over time.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Create a failure-mode list before competition: what can stop scoring, disable the robot, or consume long repair time?
  • Rank risks and spend design effort on the ones with the worst consequences/likelihood.
  • Add witness marks to fasteners and components that have historically moved.
  • Practice replacing high-risk assemblies; redesign access if the repair is too slow.
  • Store spares as complete ready-to-install subassemblies where that materially reduces repair time.
  • Inspect after repeated-cycle testing, not only after something breaks.
  • When a part fails, photograph it before repair and preserve evidence long enough to diagnose root cause.
  • Update the inspection checklist when a new failure mode appears.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
