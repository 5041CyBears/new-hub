Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Prepare the robot, software, pit, batteries, documentation, and people for event-day operation.
  • Build repeatable pre-match and post-match checklists that reduce preventable failures.
  • Use current inspection and event resources as the authority for legal and procedural requirements.
  • Manage software/configuration changes deliberately during competition.
  • Practice fast diagnosis and repair within real turnaround time.

Big Picture

The system at a glance

Inspection

Use the current FIRST inspection checklist/quick reference before the event. Perform a self-inspection early enough to fix issues, not in the inspection line.

Battery System

Number batteries, track use/charge state, inspect connectors, and establish a rotation. A robot with great mechanisms but poor battery discipline is not competition-ready.

Software Backup

Bring a known-good code commit, robot configuration backups, required development tools, cables, and the ability to deploy without relying on one person's laptop.

Pit Organization

Every frequently used tool and spare should have a home. The pit should support safe, fast work rather than becoming storage chaos.

Match Turnaround

Define what must be checked after every match: damage, fasteners, wheels, mechanism alignment, wiring, battery, software status, and game-specific consumables.

People & Roles

Queueing, battery, drive team, repair lead, scouting, judging, and pit communication need clear ownership with backups.

Core Knowledge

Important concepts

Change Control

During events, do not make untracked changes to code, configuration, firmware, or mechanism geometry. Record what changed and verify the robot before queueing.

Spare Strategy

Prioritize high-wear, high-risk, hard-to-source, and fast-replaceable spares. A complete spare robot is rarely practical; a spare critical assembly often is.

Known-Good State

Mark/calibrate mechanism positions, record firmware/configuration, and keep a known-good software tag/commit so recovery is possible.

Tool Access

The fastest repair is one the team already practiced with the correct tool and spare. Do not bury critical hex keys, hubs, cables, or wheels.

Communication

Use short status language: 'robot safe,' 'battery changed,' 'intake repaired,' 'code unchanged,' 'ready for systems check.'

Event Learning

Track failures, repair time, and causes. Competition is a high-quality stress test that should improve the next build.

Practical Advice

What students should actually do

  • Run a complete self-inspection before leaving for the event and again after major robot changes.
  • Practice replacing your most failure-prone assembly with a stopwatch.
  • Use paint marks/witness marks where they make loosening or movement easy to detect.
  • Assign one person to approve/record competition software changes.
  • After a repair, run a short defined systems check instead of only testing the repaired motor.
  • Charge batteries with enough time to cool/settle as appropriate and keep a clear rotation/log.
  • Create a 'leave pit' checklist for Driver Station, gamepads, battery, robot configuration, mechanism positions, and required field items.
  • Do not let scouting/judging responsibilities strip the pit of every student who can repair or program the robot.

Competition Habits

Make the system repeatable and recoverable

Make state visible

Use labels, telemetry, logs, marks, checklists, or diagrams so students can tell what is happening without guessing.

Test failure modes

Create controlled bad conditions and practice safe recovery instead of testing only perfect operation.

Train backups

More than one student should know how to operate, explain, troubleshoot, and repair the system.
