Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Explain how belts, pulleys, chains, and sprockets transmit motion on an FRC robot.
  • Compare belts and chain for load, maintenance, noise, repairability, and packaging.
  • Use pitch, tooth count, sprocket size, pulley size, and center distance in basic design decisions.
  • Identify common uses for 3 mm belts, 5 mm HTD belts, #25 chain, and #35 chain.
  • Choose COTS or custom components based on mechanism needs and risk.
  • Describe safe and reliable 3D printed pulley practices.

Core Vocabulary

Know the parts before designing the run

Pulley

A wheel with teeth or grooves that drives a belt.

Belt

A flexible loop that transmits rotation between pulleys.

Sprocket

A toothed wheel that drives roller chain.

Chain

A linked loop that rides on sprockets to move torque.

Pitch

Spacing between teeth or chain pins. Parts must match pitch.

Center Distance

Distance between shaft centers. Belts especially depend on this being planned.

Wrap

How many teeth engage around the pulley or sprocket. More wrap usually reduces skipping.

Basic Physics

Ratios trade speed and torque

Ratio

Ratio = Driven Teeth ÷ Driver Teeth

A larger driven pulley or sprocket slows the output and increases available torque.

Power Tradeoff

Ignoring losses, power is roughly conserved: more torque usually means less angular velocity.

Power ≈ Torque × Angular Velocity

25T driving 50T gives a 2:1 reduction: about 2× torque and 1/2 speed

Belts and Pulleys

Belts are quiet, light, and low maintenance

Best For

Shooters, intakes, conveyors, indexers, compact runs, and mechanisms where low backlash matters.

Main Advantages

Quiet, clean, efficient, no lubrication, low stretch, and smooth motion when aligned correctly.

Main Concern

Belts need correct center distance, enough wrap, enough tension, and matching pulley pitch.

Timing belt teeth interlock with pulley teeth, so the system should not slip during normal operation.

3 mm Belts

Use 3 mm belts for compact, lower-load motion

Good Uses

  • Small intakes and indexers.
  • Sensor wheels or light rollers.
  • Low-load conveyors and prototypes.
  • Mechanisms where packaging is tight.

Avoid Use In

  • Drivetrains
  • Climbers
  • Heavy arms
  • Anything that cannot tolerate tooth skipping.

3 mm Design Checks

Match 3 mm belts with compatible pulleys, use flanges where the belt may walk, and keep shafts parallel.

5 mm HTD Belts

Use 5 mm belts for stronger FRC mechanisms

9 mm Width

Useful for many medium-duty intakes, rollers, conveyors, and some arms when loads are controlled.

15 mm Width

Better for heavier loads, drivetrains, larger arms, elevators, and mechanisms where skipping would be costly.

Design Around Belt Length

Belts are fixed length, so choose pulleys and center distance together in CAD.

Do Not Mix

3 mm belts do not work on 5 mm pulleys. HTD belts need compatible HTD pulleys.

3D Printed Pulleys

Printed pulleys are powerful prototyping tools

Good Uses

Custom tooth count, custom width, integrated roller shape, light duty intake, conveyor, or prototype.

Safer Build Pattern

Print the pulley body and bolt it to a COTS hub so the shaft connection is metal and reliable.

Competition Caution

Test under real load and bring spares. Avoid printed pulleys in high-shock or hard-to-repair systems.

Use the correct tooth profile, strong material, adequate tooth thickness, flanges when needed, and inserts or through-bolts instead of weak plastic threads.

Chains and Sprockets

Chain is repairable, robust, and forgiving

Best For

Drivetrains, elevators, arms, climbers, intakes, and mechanisms that need easy repair or adjustment.

Main Advantages

Easy to shorten, strong, widely available, familiar, and tolerant of design changes.

Main Concern

Chain can stretch, derail, make noise, add backlash, and need tensioning or alignment checks.

#25 Chain

#25 chain is common for lighter and medium-duty FRC mechanisms

Good Uses

  • Intakes and conveyors.
  • Indexers and rollers.
  • Elevators and medium arms.
  • Some drivetrains when designed well.

Advantages

Smaller, lighter, easier to package, and common in modern FRC build systems.

Watch Out

It is less forgiving than #35 if alignment is poor, shock loads are high, or small sprockets are overloaded.

Design Checks

Use matching #25 sprockets, align sprockets in the same plane, and keep tension neither too loose nor too tight.

#35 Chain

#35 chain is heavier but stronger and more forgiving

Good Uses

  • Heavy drivetrains.
  • Climbers.
  • Heavy arms.
  • High shock-load mechanisms.

Advantages

Stronger, easier to inspect, and more tolerant of abuse or beginner design errors.

Tradeoff

It costs more space and weight. Larger sprockets and chain runs can be harder to package.

Design Checks

Use matching #35 sprockets, strong shaft support, and a tensioning or adjustment plan.

Robot Design Checklist

Before finalizing a belt or chain run

Match Components

Pitch, width, tooth profile, chain size, bore, shaft, and hub interface must match.

Plan Tension

Include slots, idlers, cams, adjustable mounts, or exact belt center distance.

Control Alignment

Keep shafts parallel and pulleys/sprockets in the same plane.

Support Shafts

Use bearings on both sides of high-load pulleys or sprockets when possible.

Protect the Run

Keep belts and chains away from frame edges, carpet, bumpers, wires, and hands.

Bring Spares

Pack belts, chain, master links, half links, hubs, sprockets, pulleys, and tools.
