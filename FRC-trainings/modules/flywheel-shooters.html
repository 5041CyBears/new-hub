Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Explain how a flywheel shooter stores and transfers rotational energy to a game piece.
  • Choose single-wheel, hooded, dual-wheel, and multi-wheel layouts based on shot requirements.
  • Relate wheel diameter, RPM, surface speed, compression, contact length, inertia, and recovery time to shot consistency.
  • Design a repeatable feeding path, compliant compression, guarding, and serviceable wheel/shaft support.
  • Use velocity feedback plus feedforward, logging, and controlled tests to tune a shooter instead of guessing.
  • Diagnose common shooter failures such as RPM sag, inconsistent exit angle, slip, jams, vibration, and changing shot distance.

Big Idea

A good shooter is an energy-transfer system, not just a fast motor

Store

The rotating wheel(s) store kinetic energy. More inertia reduces speed drop per shot, but usually increases spin-up time and mass.

Transfer

Compression, friction, contact length, wheel material, geometry, and feed timing determine how consistently energy reaches the game piece.

Recover

Motors and control software must restore wheel speed quickly enough for the desired shot cadence.

Practical rule: optimize for repeatable exit velocity and angle. Maximum free speed by itself does not make a good FRC shooter.
Common Architectures

Know what each layout makes easy—and what it makes hard

Single wheel + hood

Compact and common. The fixed hood reacts against the game piece and usually creates backspin.

Opposed wheels

Two powered surfaces can control exit speed and spin. Requires careful speed matching and structure.

Side-by-side wheels

Useful where the game piece is wide or needs lateral centering. Differential speed can intentionally add spin.

Staged wheels

Multiple acceleration contacts can reduce load per contact but add synchronization and packaging complexity.

Surface Speed

RPM matters because it produces wheel surface speed

Wheel surface speed: v = π × diameter × RPM ÷ 60. Keep units consistent.

The game piece will generally leave slower than the wheel surface because the contact is compliant and may slip. Use the calculation as a design/tuning reference, not a promise of exact projectile speed.

Flywheel Inertia

More inertia reduces shot-to-shot RPM drop—but costs weight and spin-up response

For a solid disk approximation: I = ½mr² and rotational energy E = ½Iω². Real wheels, hubs, shafts, and multiple rotating parts should be summed.

A steel ring near the outside radius stores more energy per unit mass than the same mass near the center. Never add rotating mass without checking retention, balance, shaft/bearing load, and safety.

Feeding Is Part of the Shooter

A consistent shooter can still miss if the piece enters differently every time

Stage the piece

Use a sensor or physical stop so the shot begins from a known position.

Wait for readiness

Require wheel speed—and often aim/robot velocity conditions—before feeding.

Control the handoff

Prevent the feeder from changing piece angle, side-load, or compression unpredictably.

Closed-Loop Velocity

Use feedforward for the predictable motor demand and feedback for the remaining error

Testing

Build a shot map with evidence, not memory

Log

  • target and measured RPM
  • battery voltage/current
  • feed time
  • robot pose/range
  • shot result

Control variables

  • same piece condition
  • same launch position
  • repeat several shots
  • change one variable at a time
  • retest after mechanical changes

Practical Advice

What experienced students should insist on before calling a shooter “done”

  • Design adjustability into prototypes: hood angle, compression, wheel speed, and feeder timing are easier to tune when they are not hard-coded into metal.
  • Use the largest practical sample of real game pieces. A shooter tuned only on one pristine piece is not tuned.
  • Record changes. “We changed the RPM until it worked” is not a development process.
  • Guard stored rotational energy. Inspect wheel hubs, keys/hex interfaces, shaft collars, retaining hardware, and cracks every event.
  • Separate aim error from shooter error. Log robot pose/targeting, RPM, and feed timing so students know what actually caused a miss.
  • Prefer a stable operating region over an extreme one. Running every motor and wheel at its limit usually reduces reliability.
