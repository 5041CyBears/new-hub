Module Goals

By the end, students should be able to:

  • Compare common FRC linear-motion methods including elevators, belts, chain, cable/drum, rack-and-pinion, and lead screws.
  • Design elevator stages around load paths, bearings, overlap, anti-racking structure, rigging, hard stops, and service access.
  • Relate motor torque, drum/sprocket radius, gearing, speed, and load to linear force and velocity.
  • Use limit switches, absolute/relative sensing, homing, soft limits, motion profiles, PID, and ElevatorFeedforward safely.
  • Recognize cascade/continuous rigging concepts and verify actual travel ratios rather than copying geometry blindly.
  • Diagnose binding, racking, cable damage, skipped belts/chain, sensor faults, and uncontrolled descent.

Linear Motion Choices

Choose the mechanism around load, travel, packaging, backdrivability, and maintenance

Elevator stages

Nested rigid stages provide long vertical travel in a compact footprint. Require bearing alignment, overlap, and rigging.

Rack / belt / chain

Direct positive drive can be simple and repeatable. Tension, support, and debris tolerance matter.

Lead screw

Useful for shorter, high-force, precise travel; often slower and may be self-locking depending on geometry.

Elevator Structure

The drive system cannot fix a flexible or misaligned elevator

Guide the load

  • Bearings or rollers constrain the intended degrees of freedom.
  • Place guides far enough apart to resist moment loads.
  • Keep rails parallel and square.

Preserve overlap

  • Stages need enough engagement to remain stiff.
  • Hard stops must prevent rollers from leaving their tracks.
  • Check worst-case load at full extension.

Force & Speed

Start with the drum/sprocket and work backward through gearing

Linear speed = circumference × shaft RPM ÷ 60
Ideal line force = shaft torque ÷ drum radius

Real systems lose force to friction and efficiency. Add margin for acceleration, shock, misalignment, and battery sag.

Rigging

Continuous and cascade systems move stages differently

Do not memorize a rope diagram without understanding it. Trace every cable/belt segment and calculate how much each moving attachment changes length. Verify the resulting stage and carriage travel in CAD and by hand before cutting rope.

Continuous-style rigging

A powered line can route through moving pulleys so later stages move as earlier stages extend.

Cascade-style rigging

Stages may be linked so each stage is driven relative to the preceding stage. Packaging and load distribution differ.

Sensors, Homing & Limits

An elevator must know where it is—and what to do when that knowledge is wrong

Relative encoder

Excellent for motion after a known zero. Loses absolute reference across some resets.

Absolute sensor

Can preserve position reference, but installation range and mechanism coupling must be reliable.

Limit switch / hard stop

Useful for homing and protection. Do not use a destructive collision as your normal zeroing strategy.

Motion Profiles & Gravity

Smooth setpoints reduce shock loads and make control easier

A trapezoidal profile limits velocity and acceleration. ProfiledPIDController can generate profile setpoints while PID tracks them, and ElevatorFeedforward models static friction, gravity, velocity, and acceleration voltage.

Practical Advice

Elevators usually fail at interfaces

  • Design the structure and bearings first; then route the drive through a geometry that does not side-load the stages.
  • Use symmetric loads or mechanically synchronize sides when possible. One drive point on a wide carriage invites racking.
  • Protect sensor wires and cable carriers through the full motion range.
  • Make tension adjustments accessible without disassembling the entire elevator.
  • Plan safe maintenance supports. Never trust motor holding torque while hands are inside a raised mechanism.
  • Log current alongside position. Rising current at the same location is often an early sign of binding or damage.
